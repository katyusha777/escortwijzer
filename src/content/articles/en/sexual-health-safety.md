In Dutch sex work a condom is the norm — the default, not a negotiable extra, and whoever tries to skip it (client or worker) is the red flag. STI testing is free and can be anonymous at the GGD if you are under 25 or in a higher-risk group, and available through your GP otherwise; for anything specific, go to [Soa Aids Nederland](https://www.soaaids.nl/en/sti-test) or the GGD, because this is an information page, not medical advice.

That is the short answer. The rest covers why that condom norm exists, how testing in the Netherlands actually works, what PrEP does and does not cover, and why a professional's boundaries around safer sex are a legitimacy signal rather than a downside.

## Why is a condom the standard, not something to negotiate?

Because that is simply how Dutch sex work operates: the condom is the default. Soa Aids Nederland puts the basics plainly — having sex with a condom makes an STI less likely ([soaaids.nl](https://www.soaaids.nl/nl/fijne-gezonde-seks/condooms)). For someone doing this work day in, day out, consistent condom use is not prudishness; it is routine risk management.

That sets the direction of the red flag. A professional insisting on a condom is doing nothing unusual — she is following the norm. The person trying to get around it — whether a client asking, or an ad offering "without" — is the one departing from how the market is supposed to work. Insisting on safer sex is normal behaviour, not something to escape.

## What does a "bareback" or "no condom" ad mean?

It is a warning sign, and a double one. Someone advertising condomless service takes that risk with every client — that is the health part. But it also says something about professionalism: a worker who openly drops the norm is showing that the usual boundaries are absent.

My position here, with a reason: **a worker who holds firm on condoms is a legitimacy signal, not a downside.** Fixed, non-negotiable boundaries around safer sex mean someone who works to a standard and expects to do so again next week — the opposite of a red flag. A "no condom" claim is the inverse, and it usually travels with the other markers of a fake or best-avoided profile. How to spot those is in our guide to [spotting fake escort profiles](/en/guides/spot-fake-escort-profiles/).

## Where and when do you test for STIs in the Netherlands?

Two main places: your GP (huisarts) or a GGD STI clinic (Soa-poli). There are also reliable home tests, which Soa Aids Nederland says are as reliable as a test at the GP or GGD ([soaaids.nl](https://www.soaaids.nl/en/sti-test)).

When? If you have sex with different partners, testing occasionally is sensible — even without symptoms, and especially if a condom was skipped at some point. If you have symptoms, or you hear a partner has an STI, that is a clear moment to go; tell the doctor what is going on so they know which tests you need.

A practical note for visitors: this is Dutch healthcare, not a walk-in you fit between two appointments. A GGD appointment is planned ahead — and cannot be booked by email.

## Who can test for free and anonymously at the GGD?

Not everyone — and this is the part people most often get wrong. The Dutch government lists the groups who can use the GGD for a free (and, if wanted, anonymous) STI test: among others people under 25, sex workers, men who have sex with men, people with symptoms, people whose sexual partner has an STI, and people from areas with higher STI rates ([rijksoverheid.nl](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/onderwerpen/gezondheid-en-preventie/seksuele-gezondheid/seksuele-gezondheidszorg)).

If you fall outside those groups, you test through the GP. That can cost money, because it goes via your health insurance and counts toward your yearly deductible (eigen risico). So free GGD testing exists for those at highest risk or facing the highest barrier; everyone else uses regular care.

## What is Sense, and who is it for?

Sense is the GGD's youth service (sense.info) for anyone under 25. It offers free, confidential advice and STI testing, with one important detail: your GP, your insurer and, for example, your parents are not informed unless you say you want them to be ([rijksoverheid.nl](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/onderwerpen/gezondheid-en-preventie/seksuele-gezondheid/seksuele-gezondheidszorg)).

For a young client or worker who feels awkward about it, that is the lowest-barrier channel available. Chlamydia is the most common STI among young people in the Netherlands, and at Sense that test is standard.

## What does PrEP do — and what doesn't it?

PrEP is a pill that prevents HIV. Since 2024, free PrEP care is available through the GGD for people at elevated HIV risk, broadened beyond only men who have sex with men and trans people. The care is free, but you pay for the pills yourself: roughly €30 for 30 tablets, and the price varies by pharmacy ([rijksoverheid.nl](https://www.rijksoverheid.nl/onderwerpen/gezondheid-en-preventie/seksuele-gezondheid/prep-zorg-en-pillen)). Note that many GGDs have waiting lists, so it is not a decide-today-start-tomorrow thing.

And the key point: PrEP protects against HIV, not other STIs such as chlamydia, gonorrhoea or syphilis. Anyone on PrEP still needs a condom for the rest. PrEP does not replace safer sex; it fills one specific gap.

## The honest limitation: safer is not zero risk

Here is the part most guides leave out. A condom sharply reduces the chance of an STI, but it does not rule it out entirely. Soa Aids Nederland is clear about it: condoms do not fully protect against all STIs, because some — such as herpes and genital warts (HPV) — spread via skin not covered by the condom ([soaaids.nl](https://www.soaaids.nl/nl/fijne-gezonde-seks/condooms)).

That does not make a condom any less the norm; it makes occasional testing the sensible complement. Safer sex lowers the risk substantially — it does not set it to zero — and that is exactly why testing belongs alongside it.
