Yes, escorts are legal in the Netherlands. Sex work by consenting adults is a recognised profession, and paying for it is not a crime for the client. The Dutch government states it plainly: prostitution is legal as long as it involves sex between consenting adults. In 2000 the Netherlands became the first country in Europe to take the running of sex businesses out of criminal law — brothels, clubs and escort agencies have been legal, licensed businesses ever since.

Legal does not mean unregulated. What is allowed depends on national criminal law plus the rules of each municipality, and those differ from city to city. This guide covers exactly where the lines are: what is illegal, how Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague and Utrecht regulate escorts, and what is changing in Dutch law in 2026.

## What exactly is illegal?

Three things: paying for sex with anyone under 18, any form of coercion or trafficking, and running a sex business without a licence where one is required.

**Minors.** Paying for sex with a 16- or 17-year-old is a crime committed by the client, even if the client did not know the age. Until 1 July 2024 this was Article 248b of the Dutch Criminal Code (Wetboek van Strafrecht, abbreviated "Sr"); under the new Sexual Offences Act it moved into Articles 245 and 246 Sr, carrying up to six years in prison (sexual acts) or nine years (penetration) — well above the four-year maximum of the old Article 248b. For children under 16, the even harsher Articles 247–250 Sr apply, with an additional sentencing uplift (Article 254 Sr) when the child was offered for payment.

**Coercion and trafficking.** Forcing, deceiving or exploiting someone into sex work is human trafficking under Article 273f Sr: up to 12 years in prison, rising to 15 years for cases involving minors or violence, 18 years where serious injury results, and 30 years or life where the victim dies. A bill modernising this article and broadening the criminalisation of labour exploitation passed the lower house of parliament on 10 June 2025 and is now before the senate.

**Unlicensed operation.** Running an escort agency, club or window brothel without the municipal operating licence required by local regulations means fines and closure.

## Since when has escort been legal in the Netherlands?

Since 1 October 2000, when the general ban on brothels was lifted. The bill (25.437, passed by the senate on 26 October 1999) replaced the ban on operating sex businesses with a ban on *involuntary* prostitution and prostitution by minors.

The aim was twofold: regulate the legal part of the industry through municipal licensing, and crack down harder on exploitation — the maximum sentence for which rose from one to six years at the time. Selling sex itself had long been tolerated in the Netherlands; what changed in 2000 was that organising it commercially (brothels, clubs, escort agencies) became a legal trade.

## Who sets the rules: the state or the city?

Both — but the practical rules mostly come from the city. Criminal law (the age limit, coercion, trafficking) is national. Licensing is set by each municipality in its APV (Algemene Plaatselijke Verordening — the local bylaw every Dutch municipality maintains).

That is why the details vary by city: how many sex businesses are allowed, whether the minimum age at licensed businesses is 18 or 21, and whether independent sex workers may receive clients at home. Almost every municipal regime shares the same core: an operating licence for escort agencies, window brothels, clubs and erotic massage parlours, an approved business plan, and an integrity screening of the operator under the Bibob Act. There is still no uniform national system — creating one was the goal of the now-abandoned sex work regulation bill (more below). In our view that patchwork is the system's main weakness — a rule that applies in Rotterdam may not apply in Utrecht — but a licensed, city-regulated industry still beats criminalisation, because inspections, tax registration and reporting channels only exist where the work is legal.

## Is escort legal in Amsterdam?

Yes. Escort agencies operate legally in Amsterdam with a municipal operating licence, and booking an escort is legal for clients. Amsterdam runs a stricter regime than the national minimum: since July 2013, sex workers at licensed businesses — including the window brothels of De Wallen (the Red Light District) — must be at least 21 rather than 18.

Operators have a formal duty of care towards every sex worker working for them and are screened under the Bibob integrity act. For clients, the practical upside of Amsterdam's regime is that booking through a licensed agency means age and identity have been checked. ## Is escort legal in Rotterdam?

Yes. Rotterdam licenses four categories of sex business, one of which is explicitly the escort agency — defined as "mediation between client and sex worker". Licensed businesses may only work with sex workers aged 21 or over; violations can cost the operator the licence.

Rotterdam's distinctive feature: independent sex workers may work from their own registered home address without a licence, provided they work alone and meet strict conditions. The city also funds support services such as the Door2Door information centre and a Humanitas hotline for reporting suspected exploitation.

## Is escort legal in The Hague?

Yes, though The Hague is the most tightly capped of the four big cities. Sex businesses — escort agencies included — need an operating licence issued one year at a time (fee: €1,704), with a Bibob integrity screening every four years. The number of sex establishments per location has been fixed since 1 October 1999: new businesses can only enter by taking over an existing licence.

Sex workers in The Hague must be at least 21 and registered as self-employed with the KvK (Kamer van Koophandel, the Dutch Chamber of Commerce). The city refers workers to SHOP, a foundation offering support, healthcare and exit programmes.

## Is escort legal in Utrecht?

Yes. Every sex business in Utrecht requires a licence, and escort agencies are treated the same as physical venues. Operators must submit a business plan that concretely describes how safety, hygiene, the prevention of trafficking and workers' autonomy are safeguarded — including sex workers' right to refuse any client.

The licence is personal, non-transferable and must fit the city's zoning plan. Managers must be at least 21.

## Can someone work as an independent escort?

Yes. Independent sex work is an ordinary self-employed profession in the Netherlands: you register with the KvK (registration fee €85.15), file VAT returns quarterly and income tax annually, and can use the small-business VAT exemption (KOR) below €20,000 in turnover. The tax office is informed automatically upon registration.

Whether a licence is also needed depends on the work form and the city: online work needs no licence anywhere, independent escorting requires a municipal licence in some cities, and receiving clients at home is banned in many places. Workers who prefer not to run a business can work at a licensed venue under the "opting-in" scheme, where the operator withholds payroll tax under an agreement with the Belastingdienst (Dutch tax office). For clients the mirror image applies: booking an independent escort is legal, and the only legal risks are age and coercion.

## What happened to the Dutch sex work regulation bill (WRS)?

It was shelved. The Wet regulering sekswerk (bill 35.715, introduced on 26 January 2021) would have created a national licensing duty for individual sex workers and operators, a minimum age of 21, a national register, and criminal liability for clients of unlicensed workers. In a letter to parliament dated 3 April 2026, the Minister of Justice and Security announced the cabinet would not proceed: it endorses the goals, but the bill contained too many contested choices.

Targeted measures are replacing it. On 13 May 2026 the cabinet announced plans to raise the legal minimum age for sex work from 18 to 21 and to examine how a ban on pimping could be given legal form. A new bill on municipal supervision of sex businesses (the Wgts) is being submitted to parliament, giving cities a firmer legal basis for oversight and data processing. Since 1 April 2026 an identity check has applied to new advertisements on sex work platforms, and funding for exit programmes has risen to €6.5 million per year. Note for 2026: the age increase is a cabinet plan, not yet law — the national minimum age is still 18.

## How does a client stay on the right side of the law?

By checking three things: age, consent, and the channel you book through. A client who takes those seriously runs virtually no legal risk in the Netherlands.

- **Age.** Never book when age is in doubt. "I didn't know" is no defence: Articles 245 and 246 Sr criminalise the client regardless of what they knew. Licensed agencies and serious platforms check ID — and since 1 April 2026 an ID check on new advertisements is mandatory.
- **Consent.** Signs of coercion — someone else handles all communication, the escort cannot say no, she has no phone of her own — are reason to walk away immediately; suspicions can be reported anonymously via Meld Misdaad Anoniem (0800-7000).

The bottom line is simple: escorts are legal in the Netherlands, and deliberately so since 1 October 2000. The law is not aimed at the client who books an adult escort working of her own free will — it targets whoever pays minors, exploits people or operates without a licence. Run the three checks above and you are on solid legal ground, in Amsterdam, Rotterdam, The Hague, Utrecht and every other Dutch municipality.
